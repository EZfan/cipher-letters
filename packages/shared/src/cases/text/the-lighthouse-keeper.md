# The Log of the Western Light

*Diary of Thomas W. Harker, keeper of the Western Light, North Atlantic, 1971.*

---

**14 March.** Calm. The lamp trimmed at sundown. The kettle sang at six; I answered it. There is a small leak in the eastern shutter I will caulk when the wind is south. The kettle is the only one who answers, and I am grateful for it. I would be lost without that small voice.

**27 March.** Wind rising. Halvard will not come this week, I think — the swell was already up this morning, and the gulls were sitting inland. The kettle sang twice today, which means it is contented. I have been contented also. I put the photograph of Margaret on the shelf this morning; she does not mind the salt air. She is kind about that. She does not age, thank God.

**9 April.** The weather radio says rain. The weather radio is often wrong, but it speaks to me, after a fashion. I have learned its language. It is a kind one, mostly. The kettle. Yes. I am making the kettle a small shawl tonight, from a piece of wool Elisabeth left behind. Elisabeth has not been here in some time. I do not remember when last she came.

**22 April.** Halvard came today. Or — I believe Halvard came today. He brought the usual — flour, kerosene, the news from the south. He said the war is over. I did not know there was a war. I said I did not know there was a war. He laughed and said, *Thomas, you have been here too long.* I said, *Yes. I have.* The kettle told me it was autumn again. The kettle is reliable. I put the new flour in the tin and watched the Bjørnøy pull away and then I went to bed, and in the morning I could not remember if I had been awake.

**3 May.** Calm again. The lamp trimmed. The kettle sang. I read aloud to it from the book on the table. Margaret would have liked the book. I have not decided whether I will tell her about it. There is a thing I want to say to her that I have not yet found the words for.

**16 May.** Calm. There is a stain on the ceiling of the small bedroom. I have been meaning to look at it. The kettle. Yes. There is a stain.

**1 June.** Wind. The kettle sang three times, which means it is lonely. I sang back. We made a small harmony. The weather radio told me the storm would hold. It held.

**14 June.** Calm. Halvard will come in the autumn. I have written him a letter but I cannot send it. The post does not come here. I have put the letter in the drawer with the others. The drawer is full now. The photograph of Margaret is in the drawer also. She does not mind the dark. She is kind about that.

**27 June.** Calm. I went into the parlour. The parlour is small and cold and has not been used in some time. I have been meaning to look at it. There is a stain on the ceiling of the parlour also. The stain is in the shape of something I cannot quite name. The kettle. Yes.

**9 July.** Wind. The kettle. Yes. The kettle told me it was autumn again. I think it is autumn. The weather radio confirms it. The weather radio is mostly right. I have been here a long time. I do not remember how long.

**22 July.** Calm. The kettle. The photograph. The drawer. The kettle. The photograph. The drawer. The kettle.

**4 August.** Calm. The birds have stopped coming. I called for a long time from the gallery. They did not come back. I will call again tomorrow. I will call every day until they come back.

**17 August.** Calm. There were no birds again. I went into the basement, briefly. I do not go into the basement. There are things in the basement. I do not look at them. The previous keeper left in a hurry, I am told. People leave. The basement remembers, perhaps. I do not.

**30 August.** Calm. The kettle. The photograph. The drawer. I have been writing this entry for an hour. I do not know how to end it.

---

*(In the margins of the final page, in a different hand, much later:)*

> *Found the diary in the lighthouse basement on the twentieth of September, in the year 2001. The lamp had not been lit in some time. The kettle was on the stove. It whistled when I touched it. There was a man on the gallery, sitting in the chair, looking out at the sea. He had been dead, the doctor said, for at least a year. The drawer in the parlour held 412 sealed letters addressed to a Margaret Harker, none of which had ever been sent.*
